US Equities & Economy

Fed policy · earnings · labor

Fed Holds at 3.50–3.75% as Hawks Gain Ground

Key takeaway

A fifth consecutive hold masks growing hawkish dissent: three officials favored a hike in July, and August’s blowout payrolls report (+162,000 vs. ~53,000 expected) reignited September hike odds to roughly 58%.

Market / sector impact

Rate-sensitive equities — growth and long-duration tech — are most exposed to a repricing; front-end Treasury yields likely stay elevated into the Sep 16–17 FOMC.

Client talking point

“Clients keep asking when rates are coming down. The honest answer right now is: probably not next. Core PCE is still running at 3.3%, hotter than the 2.5% core CPI print, and the labor market just surprised to the upside. We're managing for a Fed that may need one more hike, not one that's about to cut.”

AI Capex Conviction Meets a Monetization Clock

Key takeaway

Hyperscalers are guiding to roughly $690–800B of combined 2026 capital spending, and investors are increasingly asking how fast that spend converts into operating margin rather than just topline growth.

Market / sector impact

Mega-cap tech remains the market's single largest concentration risk; a margin-trajectory miss from any hyperscaler could trigger an outsized index-level move.

Client talking point

“The AI story hasn't broken — it's matured. We're no longer debating whether AI is real; we're asking which companies are converting capex into cash flow. That makes this a stock-picker's market inside a mega-cap-weighted index.”

European Union & UK

ECB · BoE · fiscal risk

ECB Signals More Hikes Are Coming, Not Fewer

Key takeaway

After a June hike to 2.25–2.40% and a July pause, ECB minutes explicitly warned markets not to read the pause as the end of tightening; a further move toward 2.50–2.65% is priced for the September 10 meeting.

Market / sector impact

Eurozone credit and rate-sensitive equities face headwinds even as flash PMI data (52.8 manufacturing, ~52.0 composite) shows the real economy holding up better than the hiking cycle implies.

Client talking point

“This isn't the ECB responding to recession risk — it's responding to energy-driven inflation against a resilient growth backdrop. That combination argues for staying selective in European duration rather than adding broadly.”

UK Gilts Near a 19-Year High Ahead of the Autumn Budget

Key takeaway

The 10-year gilt touched 5.29% before easing to roughly 5.16%, while the BoE held its Bank Rate at 3.75% on a split 6–3 vote with inflation running at 2.9%.

Market / sector impact

The Autumn Budget is now a live volatility event for UK rates; further fiscal slippage could retest the highs and spill into GBP.

Client talking point

“If a client holds UK gilts, this is a duration conversation, not a credit-quality one. The risk here is a fiscal event, and we're positioning shorter into the Budget date rather than guessing the outcome.”

Macro Mechanics & Transmission Channels

How policy, inflation, and currency moves actually travel through markets — not just what moved.

RatesYield-Curve Dynamics & Term Premium

Long-end yields move for two separable reasons: the market's expectation of the future policy path, and the term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration through an uncertain path, which rises independently when sovereign issuance is heavy or fiscal credibility is in question.

Right now: Bund (~3.35%), gilt (~5.16%) and JGB (3.00%) yields are all elevated even where central banks are only debating a hold-versus-hike, not accelerating cuts — a signal that term premium, not just rate-path expectations, is doing a lot of the work. A rising term premium compresses valuation multiples, hitting long-duration growth equities hardest.

InflationPPI-to-CPI Passthrough & the CPI/PCE Gap

Producer-price increases in energy, freight, and industrial inputs typically pass through to core consumer goods prices with a 2–3 month lag, as retailers reprice existing inventory before restocking at higher cost. CPI and PCE can also diverge because PCE uses a broader, chain-weighted basket (including employer-sponsored health insurance) that reweights toward substitutes faster than CPI's fixed-basket method.

Right now: July core CPI printed 2.5% y/y while core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — ran hotter at 3.3% y/y. That composition gap is wider than typical, and it's the reason the Fed can sound hawkish even when the headline CPI print looks tame.

FXSovereign Balances & Currency Resilience

A country running a current-account surplus and a large net foreign-asset position (like Japan) has structural currency support that shows up especially when global risk aversion rises. A country running a persistent current-account deficit needs continuous foreign capital inflows to fund it, which makes its currency and its bond yields more sensitive to a confidence shock.

Right now: USD/JPY has rallied toward ~156 on a narrowing rate differential and intervention speculation, while the UK's gilt-market wobble ahead of the Autumn Budget is a textbook deficit-country confidence test playing out in GBP and gilt yields simultaneously.

§2

Micro Fundamentals & Equity Mechanics

Reading through headline earnings and commodity prices to the underlying physical and accounting mechanics.

EquityEarnings Quality & CapEx Conversion

Capital spent today doesn't hit the income statement as an expense immediately — it's capitalized and depreciated over years. The analyst discipline is tracking the capex-to-depreciation ratio and free-cash-flow conversion over several quarters, not just the current quarter's revenue growth, because a revenue beat funded by spend that hasn't yet depreciated can flatter today's numbers.

Right now: with hyperscaler 2026 capex guided to roughly $690–800B, the real test of the AI investment thesis is operating-margin trajectory over the next 4–8 quarters as that spend begins to depreciate — not this quarter's topline growth.

CommoditiesBackwardation vs. Contango

Backwardation — when the spot or near-month price sits above forward-month prices — signals immediate physical scarcity: buyers are paying a premium for metal or oil delivered now because visible warehouse inventories (LME, COMEX) are drawing down. Contango, the opposite shape, signals ample supply or high storage costs, which incentivizes buyers to defer purchases.

Right now: copper's steep backwardation reflects a genuine mix of supply loss (Chile output down 9.4% y/y, a Congo export restriction) and tariff-driven strategic stockpiling in US warehouses — meaning part of the signal is positioning, not pure end-demand strength. Analysts should separate the two before extrapolating the price into a demand forecast.

Analyst Learning Notes

Standalone mechanics worth internalizing — generalizable beyond this month's headlines.

✎ Learning Note

Reading a Futures Curve Shape

Always ask two questions before trading a curve shape: is inventory actually falling (check LME/COMEX warehouse data), and is demand for immediate delivery genuinely rising, or is a large buyer front-loading purchases for reasons unrelated to consumption (tariff avoidance, strategic reserves)? Backwardation from the first cause is a demand signal; from the second, it's a positioning artifact.

✎ Learning Note

Term Premium Is Not the Same as Rate Expectations

A 10-year yield can rise even while a central bank holds rates steady, if investors demand more compensation for duration risk — from heavier debt issuance, fiscal concerns, or inflation-persistence fears. Don't read every yield move as a forecast of the next policy decision; decompose it first.

✎ Learning Note

Why CPI and PCE Can Diverge

CPI uses a fixed consumer-spending basket updated infrequently; PCE is broader, chain-weighted, and reweights toward cheaper substitutes faster. A gap this wide (2.5% core CPI vs. 3.3% core PCE) usually reflects category-weight differences — shelter and imputed healthcare costs typically matter most — and it explains why the Fed can sound more hawkish than the CPI headline suggests.

✎ Learning Note

Current Account Balances Predict Currency Fragility

A current-account surplus nation (Japan) funds itself; a deficit nation (the UK) borrows continuously from abroad to do so. That structural difference is why a UK fiscal event moves gilts and GBP together, while a similar shock in a surplus economy is more easily absorbed.

✎ Learning Note

Capex Today, Depreciation Tomorrow

A capex boom flatters revenue and sentiment immediately but only shows its true return once the depreciation schedule catches up, typically 2–4 years out. Track ROIC and free-cash-flow conversion trends now so a margin disappointment later isn't a surprise.
